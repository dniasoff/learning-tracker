Learning Tracker  ·  design session  ·  24 August 2026

One corpus,
three sources.

This morning Daniel set the app up for his son for the first time. The son is going for all of Mishnayos by his bar mitzvah. By the evening it was obvious the app believed something about his life that was not true — so we spent the evening designing the part that makes it true.

Where it started

The app assumed a straight line. His life is a confluence.

THE BIG ONE: app assumes one curriculum -> one track -> one plan -> one goal. Reality is one corpus (all Mishnayos) fed by three sources: Home (self-study, goal-bearing, scheduled), Rebbe (own masechta, no goal, as-and-when), School (own set, no goal, as-and-when). All accumulate to the same completion. Daniel — the session's first substantive entry

Everything that followed is a consequence of that one sentence. The corpus is shared; the goal is single; the ways ground gets covered are three, and two of them nobody at home controls. The coach's first useful move was to say what rebbe and school are not: they are not tracks, because a track implies a plan. They are attribution sources posting completions against one shared corpus, and exactly one track carries the goal and does the forecasting.

Three inflows, one vessel, one waterline. The design problem is not that there are three sources — it is that only one of them takes instructions.

How it went

Not a technique. A conversation.

No card decks, no six hats, no timed divergence. Daniel dumped straight into content and the session ran as a live design dialogue for 161 logged entries — an idea, a consequence, a push back, a scope lock, and round again. The good parts are the places where somebody was wrong.

  1. Daniel names the big one — and three “minor changes”

    Grey box for partial completion; reordering must discard stale backlog; enter expected weekly output from the other programs. Two of the three turn out to be the big one in disguise.

  2. The reframe: rebbe and school are not tracks

    A track implies a plan. They are attribution sources posting completions against one shared corpus. Exactly one track carries the goal and does the forecasting.

  3. The spine: the plan is derived, the events are the facts

    Backlog and today's schedule are computed from corpus + sequence + completions + goal date, never stored. Reorder, missed days and school coverage collapse into one trigger with three causes: inputs changed, recompute.

  4. The calendar problem, already solved four messages earlier

    Schools do not follow bein hazmanim; every country differs; every school differs even within a country. The coach circles inferred calendars and per-locale zmanim for three exchanges before noticing that Daniel's “40 weeks per year” had already made the model calendar-free.

  5. The double-count landmine

    Carving out Masechta Berachos because school is doing it, and subtracting 10/week for school, are two mechanisms for the same contribution. Do both naively and the home target comes out too low and he silently falls behind.

  6. Three scope locks and a governing principle

    Yom tov and bein hazmanim exist only to make sub-track estimates honest. No sub-goals — the deadline is a property of the one goal-bearing track. No deadline, no required rate. And: keep it as simple as possible, applied retroactively to everything already generated.

  7. The throwaway that reorganised the schema

    The lifetime learning record arrives almost as an aside. The coach's “completions are a set” dies on the spot, and with it the whole collision problem: school covering ground already learnt at home is not a conflict, it is chazara.

  8. Shabbos stops being a scheduling detail

    He learns at home on shabbos, so the main track has no excluded days at all. But he cannot log. The coach calls it self-healing; Daniel refuses the shrug; the app gets a shabbos mode.

  9. Sub-tracks harden

    Drag any masechta, perek or mishna in and it leaves the main track. Two types. One school sub-track per academic year, five other ongoing ones. Each track has exactly one current position.

  10. The reversal that saved the codebase

    Two masechtos on the main track must be impossible. The existing planner, task generation and sequencing are untouched; everything new lives in sub-tracks.

The dialogue, honestly

Where the coach was wrong.

Six times the analyst's own proposal was withdrawn or overruled. Each one is in the log with its correction attached, because the reversals are where the design actually got made.

The coach proposed

Completions are a set, not a list. Simplest possible model — one mishna is either done or not.

Daniel overruled

Lifetime learning record: every learning event kept globally — tap a mishna and see how many times he has learnt it and when, across his whole life. Plus a lifetime report.

What stands

An append-only event log — mishna, date, source. It cost nothing extra: the per-source velocity report already needed timestamped, attributed events. One table yields goal progress, times-learnt, history, velocity and attribution.

The coach proposed

Avoid inventing two sub-track types. Keep one form and make the exclusion line optional and collapsed.

Daniel overruled

There are two sub-track types. A school sub-track runs September to July and lasts exactly one year. Not an arbitrary window.

What stands

Two types — and the type is the simplification. Because a school year is a real fixed recurring structure, typing it lets the app fill the dates, offer “add next year”, drop the exclusion fields entirely and refuse nonsense windows. Generic-and-flexible would have made Daniel do all of that by hand.

The coach proposed

A sub-track with several masechtos gets one row per masechta, each with its own progress bar and its own next-mishna pointer.

Daniel overruled

Each track has exactly one current position. A sub-track holding several masechtos needs an order, like the main track. Only one thing is being learnt at a time per track.

What stands

Every track is an ordered sequence over assigned ground with a single current position. The home screen stays one row per track however much ground is assigned — and there is exactly one “next”, so the +1 button never has to ask which masechta.

The coach proposed

One kind of track, one kind of row. Main track and sub-tracks render identically; the deadline is the only difference.

Daniel corrected

The personal main track keeps its current behaviour — it still generates tasks and scheduled items exactly as it does today.

What stands

Withdrawn. A planned track produces tasks; an unplanned track produces a position. The presence of a plan, not the presence of a deadline, is what splits the UI. The home screen is mixed — and cheaper to build than the unified version.

The coach proposed

Rate and active-weeks estimates belong in the main track's goal settings, not on the sub-track — a sub-track has no goal of its own, so the numbers are not its property.

Daniel overruled

The whole form is: free-text name, mishnayos per week, exclusion checkboxes, adjustable weeks per year. All of it at creation, in one place.

What stands

Withdrawn. Everything about a source lives where the source is created. Model purity loses to user mental model.

The coach proposed

Shabbos capture needs no special logic — with no streaks and a derived plan, an unticked day nudges tomorrow's target up and motzei's ticks drop it straight back. Self-healing.

Daniel pushed back

If shabbos is ticked as a learning day, the inability to log on it must actually be dealt with, not shrugged off.

What stands

Shabbos mode: capture locked from candle-lighting, a read-only view of what is planned, a motzei catch-up card, and events dated to the day they were learnt. It became the most distinctive feature in the whole design.

And once, Daniel overruled Daniel

Mid-session he confirmed that the main track could hold several masechtos at once, the same as a sub-track. Later he reversed it outright — two masechtos on the main track must be impossible. That single reversal deleted an open question the coach had just raised (if the main track holds two masechtos, which one does today's task list draw from, and in what proportion?) and left the existing planner completely untouched. The main track only ever sees its remaining pool shrink and its daily target adjust — both already handled by the recompute engine.

The centre of the whole thing

One equation.

Somewhere in the middle of the session it became clear that every single thing being discussed was either an input to the numerator or an input to the denominator. Nothing else was actually being designed.

daily target = remaining  −  Σ min( rate × weeks left in window , ground left ) days to deadline
remainingThe corpus minus every event logged to date — which already includes everything school and rebbe have ticked. The past is counted as fact.
Σ min( … )Forward-only, summed over every sub-track. Ground assigned to a sub-track caps its contribution at that ground; a sub-track with no ground contributes pure quantity. One formula, two modes, no mode switch — and no way to count a source twice.
days to deadlineHe learns at home on shabbos, so the main track has no excluded days. Every calendar concept in this design lives in one place: the weeks-per-year count on a sub-track.

Why the estimates earn their place — illustrative, as worked in the session: ~4,192 mishnayos with three years to the bar mitzvah.

ignoring school & rebbe ~3.8 / day
counting them honestly ~2.2 / day

School at 10/wk × 39 weeks and rebbe at 5/wk × 44 weeks contribute roughly 1,830 mishnayos over the three years. The estimates roughly halve the apparent daily load. Without them the app demands nearly double what he actually needs — and a boy who is told to do 3.8 when 2.2 is the truth is being told, every day, that he is failing.

Run it backwards and you get the free version

Same machinery, both directions. Deadline set → solve for rate: how much per day. No deadline → solve for date: at this pace you finish in March 2029. And with no deadline you need no estimates at all, because actual observed velocity already contains school and rebbe. That is the build order: attribution plus measured velocity ships with zero configuration; the entire estimate apparatus unlocks only when a deadline exists.

The landmine it defuses

Reserving ground (“carve out Berachos, school is doing it”) and forecasting a rate (“subtract 10/week for school”) are two mechanisms for the same contribution. Do both naively and school pays in twice, the home target comes out too low, and he falls behind without the app ever saying so. The min() is what makes that impossible: with ground assigned, the rate stops adding contribution and starts predicting when the assigned ground completes.

Settled

The principles that survived the evening.

01

The plan is derived. The events are the only stored facts.

Backlog and today's schedule are computed from corpus + sequence + events + goal date, never stored. Reordering, missed days and school coverage are one trigger with three causes. Learning out of order needs no special case at all — it simply shrinks the remaining set and lowers tomorrow's target.

02

Exactly one deadline, on the main track.

No sub-goals, ever. No “finish Seder Moed before Pesach”. A sub-track has no goal and no required rate — but it does have progress. The equation is solved once, globally.

03

The main track never changes shape.

One masechta at a time, one plan, one deadline, the sequencing and task generation exactly as they are today. Everything new lives in sub-tracks. The main track only sees its remaining pool shrink.

04

Calendars touch nothing but a sub-track's weeks.

Yom tov and bein hazmanim exist solely to make a sub-track's throughput estimate honest. No term imports, no per-country school logic, no dates in the model at runtime — one rate and one week count each.

05

Chazara, not collision.

School covering ground already learnt at home is not a conflict to resolve; it is a second learning event, and a good one. Goal progress counts distinct mishnayos; the lifetime record counts all events. Nothing to dedupe, no dialog, and nothing lost.

06

Build in the order that needs no configuration.

Attribution and measured velocity work with zero setup and already project a finish date. Estimates, active weeks and rates are progressively disclosed — hidden until a deadline exists, then shown exactly when they start to mean something.

Mechanisms

Four things the app has to be able to draw.

Each of these started as a sentence in the conversation and only became real once it was drawn. They are the load-bearing pictures of the design.

1  ·  The grey box, cascading

Minor 1 in Daniel's list, and a prerequisite for everything else: completing one perek leaves the whole masechta reading unticked. He wants a grey box. The coach's contribution was to notice that partiality cascades — it is a rule for how any parent renders when its children disagree, at every level of the hierarchy, not a masechta-level fix.

Once school or rebbe cover part of a masechta, partial stops being an edge case and becomes the normal state of the tree. The same tri-state rendering is then reused in four places: the main corpus, the backfill browser, the drag-in-ground picker, and every sub-track's own progress view.

2  ·  The contribution timeline

Once a sub-track can have a window, the forecast stops being a flat rate and becomes a timeline of overlapping contribution windows — materially more accurate over a three-year horizon. School years chain one after another; an ongoing track just runs. And the holes become visible.

A future sub-track is legal and useful: “starts Sep 2027, 12/week” contributes to the forecast before it exists in reality and before it has a single tick. An uncovered stretch silently forecasts zero help and inflates the daily target — so the app names it rather than letting it sit there.

3  ·  The two ways a forecast lies

Both failure modes are quiet, both move the daily target, and they move it in opposite directions. Both are cheap to detect from data the app already holds.

The rate validates the carve-out

If a sub-track is assigned more ground than its rate can finish before the deadline, the shortfall comes back to the main track with a warning: “School will not finish Masechta Shabbos by 12 Sivan - 40 mishnayos come back to you.” Carving out ground a source never completes is the quiet way to miss the bar mitzvah.

Gap detection along the timeline

Any period between now and the deadline with no covering sub-track forecasts zero help. The app surfaces it as a question rather than an error — finished, or not yet planned? Same family as the warning on the left: both are holes in the forecast, and both are visible in data already held.

4  ·  One mishna, a whole life

The idea that arrived last and reorganised the storage model. Completions are not a set; they are an append-only event log of mishna + date + source. Goal progress counts distinct mishnayos. The lifetime record counts all events. A second learning never advances the bar mitzvah goal and always enriches the record.

Because the log carries dates, the corpus can also show what is warm and what is cold — a chazara prompt driven by the same data, with no new storage. Flagged optional against the simplicity mandate. And because the record is a lifetime record, the app outlives the goal: the bar mitzvah is one milestone in a record that keeps going.

Shabbos

It came in as a scheduling exclusion. It left as the whole point.

It entered the session as a checkbox — exclude shabbos and yom tov from the schedule. Then Daniel answered the open question about the denominator: yes, he learns at home on shabbos. Which removes the exclusion entirely — the main track has no excluded days, and the denominator is simply every day from now to the deadline — and replaces it with a much better problem. He learns, and he cannot log.

Four small pieces

  • Show the target before it comes in. The erev view says what there is to learn.
  • Freeze the on/off-target indicator for the duration. Never render “behind” on a day he cannot log.
  • A motzei catch-up card. One tap to confirm the whole lot, or adjust. It generalises to yom tov — up to three consecutive un-loggable days — with no extra design.
  • Date the events to the day learnt, not to the moment they were tapped.

The locked screen and the “show the target before shabbos” requirement turned out to be the same screen: a read-only view of what is planned, with no buttons on it. One thing to build, and the most useful possible thing to show on a locked screen. The app cannot stop a phone being used on shabbos — but it can refuse to record, which is the part it is responsible for.

Why the dating is not cosmetic

Three days of yom tov learning, all stamped with the moment they were tapped:

stamped when tapped

dated to the day learnt

The left-hand version reports a fake spike and zero yom tov learning. The lifetime record would be telling a lie about his life — and the velocity figure that decides whether he is on target would be built on it.

The same dating rule catches a bug that was already live: Daniel backfilled hundreds of already-learnt mishnayos on setup day. Stamped with the tap date, the velocity report opens with a several-hundred-mishnayos spike on day one and a permanently distorted baseline. So there are three date states — dated, motzei-backdated, and learnt before tracking started. Backfill is an opening balance: it counts in the balance, never as this month's income.

The deliverable

What actually gets built, in order.

The session was asked for a change list: minors first, then the big one. It ends with the minors turning out to be most of the big one, and the big one turning out to touch the main track not at all.

stage 1

The minors

  • Grey box tri-state at every level of the hierarchy — seder, masechta, perek, mishna. Empty, grey, checked.
  • Reorder → recompute. Any change to the sequence discards whatever is sitting in backlog or today's schedule and replaces it with today's freshly computed estimate.
  • Free tick, and tick-to-here. Browse the corpus and tick anything at any level, for out-of-schedule learning, for jumping ahead, and for backfill — which is the same screen used at a different moment.
  • Event dating. Every event stores when it was learnt: dated, motzei-backdated, or before-tracking. Before-tracking counts fully toward the goal and the lifetime record, and never toward velocity.
stage 2 — the big one

Sub-tracks

  • Two types. School year (year picker, rate, weeks) and ongoing (rate, bein hazmanim, weeks, optional end date).
  • Ordered, with one current position — structurally the main track minus the plan and the deadline.
  • Drag in ground. Any masechta, perek or mishna moves into a sub-track and out of the main track. Optional, incremental, phase two of onboarding a source.
  • Contribution maths. Forward-only: past counted as fact, future as estimate, never both.
  • Recompute engine — the one from stage 1, now with sub-track contribution in the numerator.
  • Home screen: today's tasks on top exactly as they render now, sub-track position rows beneath.
  • Add next year — clone the previous year's sub-track and shift its window.
  • Forecast guards: the over-commit warning and gap detection.
  • Reports: per-source velocity since he started, on or off target, PDF export.
  • Lifetime record: times learnt per mishna, with dates, and a lifetime report.
stage 3

Shabbos

  • Shabbos-mode lock. Capture closed from before candle-lighting until after tzeis, driven by zmanim and one location setting. Read-only, not invisible.
  • Motzei catch-up card and reminder, generalising to yom tov at no extra cost.
  • Events dated to the day learnt, so the record and the velocity report stay honest.

Throughout: the main track is structurally untouched. One masechta, one plan, one deadline, the same planner and the same sequencing. It only ever sees its remaining pool shrink and its daily target adjust.

What the evening was actually about

Five things worth keeping.

1

The three “minor changes” were the big one.

The grey box was its representation layer. Reorder-recompute was its engine. Once both existed, all that remained of the hardest feature in the app was a single column on the completion record saying where the learning came from.

Two thirds of it was specced while being called small.

2

The most throwaway idea solved the hardest problem.

The session opened worried about duplication — school covering ground already learnt at home, and what to do about it. Hours later Daniel offered the lifetime record almost as an aside. There was never a duplication problem. There was chazara, and no schema could see that until the lifetime record named it.

3

The hardest engineering problem was solved in the fourth message, and nobody noticed.

Every country's school calendar differs; every school differs within a country; no calendar can be imported, inferred or configured. The coach spent three exchanges circling it. Daniel had already ended it with “40 weeks per year” — one integer that contains every yom tov, every term break and every country, and needs no maintenance, ever.

4

The best design work was deletion.

“Only the main track has a deadline.” “This should be impossible — don't change main track.” “One school per year, five others.” “Forget yom tov.”

The feature got better every single time something was removed from it.

5

Shabbos changed genre.

It came in as a scheduling exclusion — a checkbox next to yom tov and bein hazmanim. It left as the app's most distinctive feature: the thing that shows a frum family built it.

Left on the table

Open, parked, and deliberately not built.

Still open

  • ?

    Fragmented history. One school split across three year-sub-tracks shows up as three rows in the lifetime report rather than one “school”. Acceptable, or does it need grouping later?

  • ?

    The rebbe's non-active weeks. Does the son learn with his rebbe through bein hazmanim and yom tov, or is that a different set of weeks again? The checkbox exists; the answer does not.

Parked, not rejected

  • •

    Letting the app learn the calendar. If school posts nothing for three weeks each spring, the app has discovered Pesach without knowing the word. Revisit only if the manual estimate proves too inaccurate in practice.

  • •

    Confidence intervals on the forecast, and streak-safety logic — cut under the simplicity mandate along with per-scope deadlines and any multi-option collision policy.

  • •

    Warm and cold. Because the log carries dates, the corpus could flag what was learnt recently and what was learnt once, years ago — a chazara prompt with no new storage. Offered, flagged optional, not adopted.

  • •

    One number instead of two. Stating a sub-track's estimate as a total for the year rather than a weekly rate would delete both the rate and the weeks field. Offered late, not taken.
